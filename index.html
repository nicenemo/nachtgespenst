Nachtgespenst

A night ghost for your keyboard: a Bluetooth LE and USB macro pad built on the M5Stack Core2 and CoreS3. Tap a tile on the touchscreen and the matching key, chord or media command arrives on your computer.

Set it up

What it does

Nachtgespenst turns an M5Stack touchscreen into a programmable HID device. The screen shows one grid of tiles per layer; tapping a tile sends the matching HID report to the host computer. There are 26 layers and a Locks page, shown on both devices.

  • Keys, chords and media. Keyboard and consumer-control bindings, including Ctrl/Shift/Alt/Win chords and the Meh and Hyper modifier sets.
  • Bluetooth LE and USB. Both boards speak BLE HID (NimBLE). The CoreS3 also enumerates as a USB keyboard and can route keys over USB, BLE, or both.
  • Host feedback. The Locks page shows Caps, Num and Scroll Lock as the host reports them, and the LED bar mirrors them.
  • Colour-coded keys. Keycap colour and legend are drawn in software, so they can differ on every page: tiles are coloured by kind of key and by modifier (Ctrl olive, Alt purple, Win teal), shifted legends are yellow, and each key can show an icon, a caption or a modifier badge. Physical keycaps and their printed legends cannot do that.
  • LED bar. Ten LEDs in the M5GO base show the colour of the current layer.
  • Keymaps as code. Layers are written in C++ in the style of ZMK, using HID shortcut macros generated from the USB HID Usage Tables (kp_A, cp_MUTE, lcs(...), lhyp(...)).
  • Scriptable. A line-based serial protocol lets a test tool switch pages, tap and press tiles, read the state and take screenshots. Every screenshot on this page came from it.

The story

The ZMK keymap behind the pages, and the history of the project.

The name. Nachtgespenst is inspired by Ben Aaronovitch's Rivers of London series, in particular the novella The October Man, which is set in Germany. The German word for a night ghost suits a small device that sits quietly beside the keyboard and does its work on command.
